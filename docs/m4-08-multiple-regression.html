4.8 Multiple regression

Several predictors at once: adjusting for confounders, uncovering trade-offs, and choosing models

NoteIn this lesson you will learn to
  • fit and interpret a multiple regression model: each coefficient is an effect holding the other predictors constant,
  • include a categorical predictor (dummy coding),
  • use multiple regression to adjust for a confounder,
  • see how two weak predictors can be strong together (a force–time trade-off), and model an interaction,
  • compare models with adjusted R², AIC and anova(),
  • detect multicollinearity with the VIF, and avoid coupled predictors.

⏱️ About 60 minutes. This is the longest lesson of the module. Take a break halfway if you need to!

The model

Multiple regression extends the straight line to several predictors:

\[y = b_0 + b_1 x_1 + b_2 x_2 + \dots + b_k x_k + \varepsilon\]

In R, you simply add predictors to the formula with +:

lm(y ~ x1 + x2 + x3, data = df)

The crucial change in interpretation: b₁ is the expected change in y for a 1-unit increase in x₁, while all other predictors are held constant. This is called the adjusted (or partial) effect.

The coefficients are again found by least squares, minimising \(\sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2\). With several predictors there’s no simple single formula; instead it’s written with matrices. X is the data matrix (a column of 1s for the intercept, plus one column per predictor), y the outcome vector and b the vector of coefficients:

\[\mathbf{b} = (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}\]

You never need to calculate this by hand (lm() does it), but it explains why strongly correlated predictors cause trouble (Part 4): \(\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}\) becomes almost impossible to invert. Each coefficient is tested exactly as in simple regression, \(t = b_j / SE(b_j)\), now with \(df = n - p - 1\) for p predictors, and the residual SE is \(s_e = \sqrt{SS_{residual} / (n - p - 1)}\).

Part 1 · Adjusting for a confounder

In lesson 4.3, soccer players jumped 2.5 cm higher than basketball players (p = 0.044). But they’re also 20 kg lighter, and lighter athletes jump higher. Body mass is a potential confounder: it’s related to both the “exposure” (sport) and the outcome (jump height).

A categorical predictor

First, the sport-only model:

R turns the factor sport into a dummy variable: sportSoccer is 1 for soccer players and 0 for basketball players. The first level alphabetically (Basketball) is the reference. So:

  • Intercept (0.381 m) = mean jump height of the reference group (basketball),
  • sportSoccer (0.025 m) = difference soccer − basketball. That’s the same 2.5 cm and the same p as the t-test!

(A t-test is just a regression with one binary predictor.)

Fit a model with both sport and body_mass_kg as predictors of jump height. Store it in model_adj and extract the coefficient table coefs with summary(model_adj)$coefficients.

lm(jump_height_m ~ sport + body_mass_kg, data = squad)

model_adj <- lm(jump_height_m ~ sport + body_mass_kg, data = squad) coefs <- summary(model_adj)$coefficients round(coefs, 4) 
model_adj <- lm(jump_height_m ~ sport + body_mass_kg, data = squad)
coefs <- summary(model_adj)$coefficients
round(coefs, 4)

Interpretation: “Soccer players jump higher” was largely explained by them being lighter. Comparing a basketball and a soccer player of the same body mass, the model predicts almost no difference. The sport effect was confounded by body mass.

You can visualise the adjusted model: it’s two parallel lines, one per sport, with the same slope for body mass:

WarningAdjustment is not magic

Regression can only adjust for confounders you measured, and only as well as the model fits. There’s also an overlap problem here: almost no basketball players are as light as most soccer players (lesson 4.4), so “the same body mass” is partly an extrapolation. Observational data can suggest, but rarely prove, causal effects.

In lm(jump_height_m ~ sport + body_mass_kg), how do you interpret the body-mass coefficient of −0.0017 m/kg?

It’s an average, adjusted (partial) association, holding sport constant. It isn’t a causal effect for an individual, and not a proportion of variance.

Part 2 · Two weak predictors, one strong model

Here’s a puzzle. Physics says that take-off velocity comes from the net impulse, i.e. the average net force multiplied by the time it acts (lesson 4.6). So both mean propulsive force and propulsion time should matter. But look at them one by one:

Only 10 % and 5 % of the variance explained! Now look at how the two predictors relate to each other:

r = −0.83: a strong trade-off. Athletes who push off quickly produce high forces; athletes with long push-offs produce lower forces. Each strategy can lead to a high jump. That’s why each variable alone says little about jump height: a high force usually comes with a short time, and vice versa. Only by knowing both can you tell how big the impulse was.

Fit model_ft, predicting jump height from both con_force_rel and con_time_s, and extract its R² (r2_ft).

lm(jump_height_m ~ con_force_rel + con_time_s, data = squad)

model_ft <- lm(jump_height_m ~ con_force_rel + con_time_s, data = squad) summary(model_ft) r2_ft <- summary(model_ft)$r.squared r2_ft 
model_ft <- lm(jump_height_m ~ con_force_rel + con_time_s, data = squad)
summary(model_ft)

r2_ft <- summary(model_ft)$r.squared
r2_ft

This phenomenon, where adding a predictor increases the importance of another, is called suppression. It happens when predictors are correlated with each other in the opposite direction to their effects on the outcome. It’s a powerful reminder that simple correlations can be misleading when predictors are related.

Interactions: when effects multiply

Physics says velocity = (force − g) × time: the effects multiply rather than add. A model with + assumes the force effect is the same at every propulsion time. An interaction term lets the effect of one predictor depend on the other. The model becomes

\[y = b_0 + b_1 x_1 + b_2 x_2 + b_3\, (x_1 \times x_2) + \varepsilon\]

so the slope of \(x_1\) is no longer a constant but \(b_1 + b_3 x_2\): it depends on the value of \(x_2\). In R, x1 * x2 means x1 + x2 + x1:x2 (both main effects plus their product):

R² rises from 0.87 to 0.95. And what’s left over (5 %) is mostly measurement and processing noise, plus the fact that h depends on v² rather than v.

ImportantRemember mathematical coupling

Why can force and time explain 95 % of jump height? Because jump height is calculated from the same force–time curve: force × time is impulse, and impulse is take-off velocity. This model is a nice demonstration of physics and of a force–time strategy trade-off, but it’s not a discovery that “force and time determine jumping ability”. They define it.

The informative questions are about the strategy: which athletes rely on force, which on time, and does that relate to sport, injury or training? Avoid putting variables like take-off velocity, net impulse or power into a model predicting jump height: you’d just be predicting jump height from itself.

Mean propulsive force alone explains 10 % of jump height, propulsion time alone 5 %, and both together 87 %. What’s the best explanation?

This is suppression. Adding any predictor increases R² a little, but a jump from 0.10 to 0.87 only happens because the second predictor corrects the first.

Part 3 · Choosing between models

Adding predictors always increases R² a little, even if the new predictor is pure noise. To compare models fairly:

  • Adjusted R² penalises for the number of predictors p; it only increases if a new predictor helps more than chance would: \[R^2_{adj} = 1 - (1 - R^2)\,\frac{n - 1}{n - p - 1}\]
  • AIC (Akaike information criterion): lower is better; it balances fit (the maximised likelihood \(\hat{L}\)) against complexity (the number of estimated parameters q): \[AIC = 2q - 2\ln(\hat{L})\]
  • anova(model_small, model_big): an F-test of whether the extra predictors in a nested model (the big one contains all predictors of the small one) significantly reduce the residual sum of squares. With \(p_s\) and \(p_b\) predictors: \[F = \frac{(SS_{res,small} - SS_{res,big}) / (p_b - p_s)}{SS_{res,big} / (n - p_b - 1)}, \qquad df = (p_b - p_s,\; n - p_b - 1)\]

Does countermovement depth (cmj_depth_m) add information beyond force and time?

  1. Fit m1 (force + time) and m2 (force + time + depth).
  2. Compare them with anova(m1, m2) and store the result in comparison.
  3. Get the adjusted R² of both (adj1, adj2).

Add cmj_depth_m to m2, then anova(m1, m2) and summary(m2)$adj.r.squared.

m1 <- lm(jump_height_m ~ con_force_rel + con_time_s, data = squad) m2 <- lm(jump_height_m ~ con_force_rel + con_time_s + cmj_depth_m, data = squad) comparison <- anova(m1, m2) comparison adj1 <- summary(m1)$adj.r.squared adj2 <- summary(m2)$adj.r.squared c(adj1 = adj1, adj2 = adj2) 
m1 <- lm(jump_height_m ~ con_force_rel + con_time_s, data = squad)
m2 <- lm(jump_height_m ~ con_force_rel + con_time_s + cmj_depth_m, data = squad)

comparison <- anova(m1, m2)
comparison

adj1 <- summary(m1)$adj.r.squared
adj2 <- summary(m2)$adj.r.squared
c(adj1 = adj1, adj2 = adj2)
TipHow many predictors can I use?

A common rule of thumb is at least 10–20 observations per predictor. With 84 athletes, a model with 4–8 predictors is reasonable; with 20 athletes, 1–2. Too many predictors overfit: the model describes the noise of your sample and predicts new athletes badly. Choose predictors based on theory, not by trying everything (remember the multiple-comparisons problem).

Part 4 · Multicollinearity

When predictors are strongly correlated with each other, the model struggles to separate their effects: coefficients become unstable and their standard errors inflate. Taller athletes are heavier (r = 0.84):

Adding height almost doubles the standard error of the body-mass coefficient, and its p-value goes from 0.0005 to 0.056, although body mass is just as relevant as before. Height itself adds nothing.

The variance inflation factor (VIF) quantifies this. For predictor j:

\[VIF_j = \frac{1}{1 - R^2_j}\]

where R²j comes from regressing predictor j on all the other predictors. VIF = 1 means no collinearity; VIF > 5 (some say > 10) is a warning sign. (The car package’s vif() calculates it for all predictors at once.)

Calculate the VIF of body_mass_kg in the model with height_m:

  1. Regress body_mass_kg on height_m and get its R² (r2_j).
  2. Calculate vif_mass.

lm(body_mass_kg ~ height_m, data = squad), then vif_mass <- 1 / (1 - r2_j).

r2_j <- summary(lm(body_mass_kg ~ height_m, data = squad))$r.squared vif_mass <- 1 / (1 - r2_j) vif_mass 
r2_j <- summary(lm(body_mass_kg ~ height_m, data = squad))$r.squared
vif_mass <- 1 / (1 - r2_j)
vif_mass

You include both peak power and mean power (r = 0.83 with each other) as predictors of jump height. Mean power becomes non-significant. Should you conclude that mean power is unrelated to jump height?

A non-significant coefficient in a multiple regression means “no unique contribution given the other predictors”, not “no relationship”.

Checklist for multiple regression

  1. Choose predictors from theory, and avoid predictors that are mathematically coupled to the outcome.
  2. Plot the data first; check predictor correlations (collinearity) and VIFs.
  3. Interpret each coefficient holding the others constant, with its 95 % CI (confint(model)).
  4. Check residuals (plot(model) in RStudio).
  5. Compare models with adjusted R², AIC or anova() for nested models.
  6. Respect the sample size (10–20 observations per predictor).
  7. Remember: adjusted associations in observational data are not proof of causation.

Lesson summary

  • lm(y ~ x1 + x2, data = df); each b is an effect holding other predictors constant.
  • Factors become dummy variables compared with a reference level.
  • Adjusting for confounders can change conclusions (sport effect → body-mass effect).
  • Correlated predictors can create suppression (force + time) or multicollinearity (mass + height, VIF).
  • x1 * x2 adds an interaction.
  • Compare models with adjusted R², AIC, anova(m1, m2).
