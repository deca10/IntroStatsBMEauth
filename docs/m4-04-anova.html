4.4 Comparing more than two groups

One-way ANOVA, post-hoc tests and eta squared

NoteIn this lesson you will learn to
  • explain why multiple t-tests are a problem when comparing 3+ groups,
  • run a one-way ANOVA with aov() and read its table (F, p),
  • find which groups differ with Tukey’s post-hoc test,
  • report the effect size η² (eta squared), and check the assumptions.

⏱️ About 40 minutes.

Three groups: light, medium and heavy athletes

In the previous lessons, body mass kept appearing as a factor in jump height. To compare three groups, the squad has been split into body-mass tertiles (thirds) with cut() and quantile() (lesson 2.3):

Why not three t-tests?

You could compare Light–Medium, Light–Heavy and Medium–Heavy with three t-tests. But each test has a 5 % chance of a false positive, so the chance of at least one false positive across three tests is about \(1 - 0.95^3 = 14\%\). With 5 groups (10 comparisons) it’s 40 %!

ANOVA (analysis of variance) tests all groups at once:

  • H₀: all population means are equal (μ_Light = μ_Medium = μ_Heavy),
  • H₁: at least one mean differs.

How ANOVA works

ANOVA compares two sources of variability:

  • between groups: how far the group means are from the overall mean,
  • within groups: how far the individual athletes are from their own group mean (the “noise”).

The ratio is the F-statistic:

\[F = \frac{\text{variance between groups}}{\text{variance within groups}}\]

In formulas, with k groups, \(n_j\) observations in group j, N observations in total, group means \(\bar{y}_j\) and grand mean \(\bar{y}\):

\[SS_{between} = \sum_{j=1}^{k} n_j (\bar{y}_j - \bar{y})^2 \qquad SS_{within} = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \sum_{i=1}^{n_j} (y_{ij} - \bar{y}_j)^2\]

\[SS_{total} = SS_{between} + SS_{within}\]

\[MS_{between} = \frac{SS_{between}}{k - 1} \qquad MS_{within} = \frac{SS_{within}}{N - k}\]

\[F = \frac{MS_{between}}{MS_{within}}, \qquad df = (k - 1,\; N - k)\]

The p-value is the probability of an F at least this large under H₀, from the F-distribution with those two degrees of freedom.

If H₀ is true, the group means differ only by chance and F ≈ 1. A large F means the groups differ more than chance would explain.

One-way ANOVA in R

Fit the model with aov(outcome ~ group, data = ...) and show the ANOVA table with summary():

The table shows, for the groups (mass_group) and the residual (within-group) variation: degrees of freedom (Df), sums of squares (Sum Sq), mean squares (Mean Sq = Sum Sq / Df), the F value (= Mean Sq groups / Mean Sq residuals) and the p-value.

Here F(2, 81) = 4.70, p = 0.012: the mean jump height differs between the body-mass groups.

Post-hoc tests: which groups differ?

A significant ANOVA says some means differ, not which. Post-hoc tests compare all pairs while controlling the overall false-positive rate. Tukey’s HSD (honestly significant difference) is the standard choice. For each pair of groups a and b it calculates the studentised range statistic

\[q = \frac{|\bar{y}_a - \bar{y}_b|}{\sqrt{\dfrac{MS_{within}}{2}\left(\dfrac{1}{n_a} + \dfrac{1}{n_b}\right)}}\]

and compares it with the studentised range distribution for k groups and N − k degrees of freedom. That distribution accounts for the number of comparisons, so the adjusted p-values and CIs keep the overall false-positive rate at 5 %.

For each pair you get the difference (diff), its adjusted 95 % CI (lwr, upr) and the adjusted p-value (p adj). Only Heavy vs. Light is significant (−4.2 cm, p = 0.013); Heavy vs. Medium is borderline (p = 0.06).

An ANOVA comparing four training groups gives p = 0.003. What can you conclude?

The ANOVA’s H₁ is “not all means are equal”. Use Tukey’s HSD (or planned comparisons) to locate the differences.

Effect size: η² (eta squared)

The p-value doesn’t say how important the group differences are. η² is the proportion of the total variability in the outcome explained by the groups:

\[\eta^2 = \frac{SS_{between}}{SS_{total}} = \frac{SS_{between}}{SS_{between} + SS_{within}}\]

Rough benchmarks: 0.01 small, 0.06 medium, 0.14 large.

Calculate η² for the body-mass ANOVA. The sums of squares are in the ANOVA table: summary(model)[[1]]$"Sum Sq" gives a vector with SS_between (first) and SS_within (second).

eta_sq <- ss[1] / sum(ss)

model <- aov(jump_height_m ~ mass_group, data = squad) ss <- summary(model)[[1]]$"Sum Sq" ss eta_sq <- ss[1] / sum(ss) eta_sq 
model <- aov(jump_height_m ~ mass_group, data = squad)
ss <- summary(model)[[1]]$"Sum Sq"
ss

Assumptions

One-way ANOVA assumes:

  1. independent observations,
  2. roughly normal residuals in each group,
  3. equal variances across groups (homogeneity of variance).

Check the residuals visually, and compare the group SDs. A common rule of thumb: the largest SD shouldn’t be more than about twice the smallest.

If the assumptions are badly violated:

  • unequal variances → Welch’s ANOVA: oneway.test(y ~ group, data = ...). Each group is weighted by its precision, \(w_j = n_j / s_j^2\), and the between-group variability is measured around the weighted mean \(\bar{y}_w = \sum w_j \bar{y}_j / \sum w_j\): \[F_W = \frac{\dfrac{1}{k-1}\sum_j w_j (\bar{y}_j - \bar{y}_w)^2}{1 + \dfrac{2(k-2)}{k^2 - 1}\sum_j \dfrac{(1 - w_j/\sum w)^2}{n_j - 1}}\] (with adjusted, non-integer denominator degrees of freedom, as in the Welch t-test),
  • non-normal / ordinal data → Kruskal–Wallis test: kruskal.test(y ~ group, data = ...). All N values are ranked together; with \(R_j\) the rank sum of group j: \[H = \frac{12}{N(N+1)} \sum_{j=1}^{k} \frac{R_j^2}{n_j} - 3(N+1), \qquad df = k - 1\]

What does TukeyHSD() do that three separate t-tests don’t?

Without adjustment, the family-wise error rate grows with every extra comparison. Tukey’s method controls it.

You compare the sprint times of two groups with an ANOVA instead of a t-test. What happens?

With two groups, one-way ANOVA and the equal-variance t-test are mathematically identical. ANOVA is the generalisation to more groups.

Lesson summary

  • 3+ groups: one-way ANOVA, summary(aov(y ~ group, data = df)). Report F(df₁, df₂) and p.
  • F = between-group variance / within-group variance.
  • Follow a significant ANOVA with TukeyHSD(model).
  • Effect size η² = SS_between / SS_total.
  • Alternatives: oneway.test() (unequal variances), kruskal.test() (non-parametric).
