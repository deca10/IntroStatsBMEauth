2.4 The normal distribution and the central limit theorem

The bell curve, z-scores, and why averages behave so nicely

NoteIn this lesson you will learn to
  • describe the normal distribution and the 68–95–99.7 rule,
  • calculate and interpret z-scores,
  • check whether data look normal with a histogram and a Q-Q plot,
  • explain the central limit theorem (CLT) and the standard error, and see them in action with a simulation.

⏱️ About 45 minutes. This lesson uses the mean and SD from lesson 2.3, so make sure you’re comfortable with them.

The normal distribution

Many measurements in biology and sport, such as body height, the small fluctuations of a force signal, or repeated measurements of the same athlete, follow a typical pattern: most values are close to the mean, and values become rarer the further you go from it, equally on both sides. This symmetric, bell-shaped pattern is the normal distribution (or Gaussian distribution).

A normal distribution is completely described by just two numbers: its mean (μ, where the peak is) and its standard deviation (σ, how wide the bell is). The height of the curve (the probability density) at a value x is

\[f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} \, e^{-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}\]

You’ll never need to calculate it by hand: R’s dnorm(x, mean, sd) does it.

dnorm() gives the height (density) of the normal curve, and stat_function() draws a function in a ggplot.

The 68–95–99.7 rule

For any normal distribution:

  • about 68 % of the values lie within 1 SD of the mean,
  • about 95 % lie within 2 SDs (more precisely 1.96),
  • about 99.7 % lie within 3 SDs.

This is why the onset threshold in lesson 2.3 used 5 SDs: if the force were just noise, a drop of more than 5 SDs would essentially never happen (less than 1 in a million samples).

z-scores

A z-score tells you how many standard deviations a value lies above (+) or below (−) the mean:

\[z = \frac{x - \bar{x}}{SD}\]

z-scores are popular in sport science for athlete profiling, because they put very different tests (jump height in cm, sprint time in s, VO₂max in mL/kg/min) on the same scale. A z of +1.5 means “1.5 SDs better than the squad average”, whatever the test.

If the data are normal, you can turn a z-score into a percentile with pnorm(), the proportion of the normal distribution below a z-score:

A basketball squad’s CMJ heights are given. Calculate every player’s z-score and store the vector in z. Then select the names of the players with a z-score above +1 and store them in top_jumpers.

z <- (squad - mean(squad)) / sd(squad), and names(z[z > 1]).

squad <- c(Alex = 38.2, Ben = 44.9, Chris = 41.0, Dimitris = 35.6, Eric = 47.3, Filip = 40.4, George = 39.1, Hugo = 42.8, Ivan = 36.7, Jon = 43.5) # z-scores z <- (squad - mean(squad)) / sd(squad) # Names of players with z above +1 top_jumpers <- names(z[z > 1]) round(z, 2) top_jumpers

Is my data normal?

Many statistical methods (t-tests, ANOVA, regression) assume that the data, or more exactly the errors, are approximately normal. Two graphical checks:

  1. A histogram: is it roughly symmetric and bell-shaped?
  2. A Q-Q plot (quantile–quantile plot): it plots the quantiles of your data against the quantiles of a normal distribution. If the data are normal, the points lie close to a straight line.

Note that a Q-Q plot uses the aesthetic sample instead of x.

A jump-height test has a squad mean of 40 cm and SD of 4 cm (approximately normal). Roughly what percentage of players jump between 32 and 48 cm?

32 and 48 cm are 2 SDs below and above the mean (40 ± 2 × 4), so about 95 % of players lie between them.

A player’s sprint time has a z-score of −1.5 relative to the squad. What does that mean?

A negative z means below the mean. For sprint times, lower is better, so z = −1.5 is fast. (Coaches often flip the sign for “lower is better” tests so that a positive z always means good.)

The central limit theorem

Now for one of the most important ideas in all of statistics.

So far we’ve looked at the distribution of individual values. Now imagine you repeatedly take a sample of n values from a population and calculate the sample mean each time. Each sample gives a slightly different mean. How are these sample means distributed?

ImportantThe central limit theorem (CLT)

If you take many random samples of size n from any population (with mean μ and SD σ), then, provided n is large enough, the sample means:

  1. are approximately normally distributed, even if the population itself is not normal,
  2. are centred on the population mean μ,
  3. have a standard deviation of \(\sigma / \sqrt{n}\), called the standard error (SE) of the mean.

A common rule of thumb is that n ≥ 30 is “large enough”, though for populations that aren’t very skewed, much smaller samples already work.

Seeing the CLT with a simulation

Let’s treat the 163 TMT-B times, which are strongly right-skewed, as our population, and simulate taking samples from it.

Two new functions:

  • sample(x, size = n, replace = TRUE) draws n random values from x,
  • replicate(1000, expression) repeats an expression 1000 times and collects the results.

set.seed() makes the random numbers reproducible: with the same seed you get the same “random” result every time.

Run the code with n <- 2, then change it to 5, 10, 30 and 60. What happens?

  • With small n, the sample means are still skewed, like the population.
  • As n grows, the histogram becomes a symmetric bell curve centred on the population mean (red line).
  • It also becomes narrower: larger samples give more precise means.

That narrowing is the standard error, σ/√n: to halve the SE, you need four times as many observations.

Why the CLT matters to you

  • It’s the foundation of inferential statistics. Because sample means are approximately normal, we can calculate confidence intervals and run t-tests on means, even when the raw data aren’t perfectly normal (Module 4).
  • It explains why we average repeated trials. If a single jump varies with SD σ around an athlete’s “true” ability, the mean of 3 jumps varies with only σ/√3 ≈ 0.58σ. Averaging trials gives a more precise estimate. That’s why test protocols ask for several jumps.
  • It explains why the normal distribution is everywhere. Many biological measurements are the sum of many small, independent influences, and sums (like means) tend to be normal.

According to the CLT, what happens to the distribution of sample means as the sample size increases?

Sample means approach a normal distribution centred on μ, with a standard error σ/√n that shrinks as n grows.

The SD of single CMJ trials of an athlete is 1.2 cm. What is the standard error of the mean of 4 trials?

SE = SD / √n = 1.2 / √4 = 1.2 / 2 = 0.6 cm.

Does the CLT say that your raw data become normal if you collect enough of it?

A common misconception! Measuring 1000 TMT-B times still gives a skewed histogram. It’s the means of repeated samples that become normal.

Lesson summary

  • The normal distribution is symmetric and bell-shaped, defined by its mean and SD; ≈68 % / 95 % / 99.7 % of values lie within 1 / 2 / 3 SDs.
  • z = (x − mean) / SD puts different tests on a common scale; pnorm(z) converts it to a percentile for normal data.
  • Check normality with a histogram and a Q-Q plot (geom_qq() + geom_qq_line()).
  • Central limit theorem: means of samples of size n are approximately normal, centred on μ, with standard error σ/√n, whatever the shape of the population.
