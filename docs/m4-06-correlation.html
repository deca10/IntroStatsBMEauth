4.6 Correlation

Measuring relationships, and recognising when a high correlation tells you nothing new

NoteIn this lesson you will learn to
  • quantify a linear relationship with Pearson’s r and test it with cor.test(),
  • interpret r² as shared variance,
  • choose Spearman’s ρ for non-linear or outlier-prone data,
  • recognise mathematical coupling: variables that correlate highly because one is calculated from the other, or because both measure the same physical quantity,
  • remember that correlation is not causation.

⏱️ About 50 minutes. This is one of the most important lessons for interpreting force-platform data.

Pearson’s correlation coefficient

The correlation coefficient r measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship between two numeric variables:

  • r = +1: perfect positive linear relationship,
  • r = 0: no linear relationship,
  • r = −1: perfect negative linear relationship.

It’s the covariance of x and y (how much they vary together) divided by the product of their standard deviations, which makes it unit-free:

\[r = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \bar{x})^2}\,\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \bar{y})^2}} = \frac{\text{cov}(x, y)}{s_x \, s_y}\]

Each product \((x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})\) is positive when an athlete is above (or below) average on both variables, and negative when they’re above on one and below on the other.

cor(x, y) gives r; cor.test(x, y) also gives a p-value (H₀: ρ = 0, no correlation in the population) and a 95 % CI. The test statistic is

\[t = \frac{r\sqrt{n - 2}}{\sqrt{1 - r^2}}, \qquad df = n - 2\]

and the CI is calculated on the Fisher z scale, where the sampling distribution of r is approximately normal, then transformed back:

\[z = \tfrac{1}{2}\ln\!\left(\frac{1 + r}{1 - r}\right), \qquad SE_z = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n - 3}}\]

\[95\%\ CI_z = z \pm 1.96 \times SE_z\]

r² = shared variance

Squaring r gives the proportion of variance that the two variables share. Peak power and jump height have r = 0.83, so r² = 0.69: 69 % of the variability in jump height is shared with peak power. Mean propulsive force (r = 0.31) shares only r² = 10 %.

Rough guide for |r| in sport science: < 0.1 trivial, 0.1–0.3 small, 0.3–0.5 moderate, 0.5–0.7 large, 0.7–0.9 very large, > 0.9 nearly perfect. But as you’ll see below, how two variables were obtained matters more than the size of r.

Test the correlation between contraction time (contraction_time_s) and jump height. Store the test in res, and calculate the shared variance r2 from res$estimate.

cor.test(squad$contraction_time_s, squad$jump_height_m), and r2 <- res$estimate^2.

res <- cor.test(squad$contraction_time_s, squad$jump_height_m) res r2 <- res$estimate^2 r2

Pearson or Spearman?

Pearson’s r assumes a linear relationship and is sensitive to outliers. Spearman’s ρ (rho) correlates the ranks of the values instead, so it captures any monotonic relationship (consistently increasing or decreasing) and is robust to outliers. It’s also the right choice for ordinal data. If there are no ties, with \(d_i\) the difference between the two ranks of observation i:

\[\rho = 1 - \frac{6 \sum_{i=1}^{n} d_i^2}{n(n^2 - 1)}\]

(With ties, ρ is simply Pearson’s r calculated on the ranks.)

When a high correlation tells you nothing new: mathematical coupling

Here is the most important idea in this lesson. A force platform produces dozens of variables from one force–time curve: jump height, take-off velocity, impulse, power, RSI… They are not independent measurements. Many are calculated from each other or are linked by the laws of physics. Correlating them can produce impressive r values that reflect definitions and physics, not physiology.

Case 1: one variable calculated from the other

Jump height h is calculated from the take-off velocity vTO (or vice versa) with the projectile equation:

\[h = \frac{v_{TO}^2}{2g} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad v_{TO} = \sqrt{2 g h}\]

So if you know one, you know the other exactly. Their “correlation” is just the curvature of a square-root function:

r = 0.999. Reporting “take-off velocity was very strongly correlated with jump height (r = 0.999, p < 0.001)” would be a correlation of a variable with itself. They contain the same variance.

Case 2: two routes to the same physical quantity

The impulse–momentum theorem (Newton’s second law integrated over time) says that the net impulse applied during propulsion equals the change in momentum:

\[J_{net} = \int (F - mg)\,dt = m \cdot v_{TO} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{J_{net}}{m} = v_{TO}\]

In words: net impulse per kg is the take-off velocity, expressed in different units (N·s/kg = m/s). In the squad data, con_impulse_rel is the propulsive impulse per kg including the part that just supports body weight, so the net impulse per kg is:

\[\frac{J_{net}}{m} = \text{con\_impulse\_rel} - g \times \text{con\_time\_s}\]

  1. Calculate the take-off velocity v_to from jump height.
  2. Calculate the net propulsive impulse per kg net_imp from con_impulse_rel and con_time_s.
  3. Calculate the correlation between net_imp and v_to (r_physics), and between the gross impulse con_impulse_rel and v_to (r_gross).
  1. sqrt(2 * g * squad$jump_height_m)
  2. squad$con_impulse_rel - g * squad$con_time_s
  3. cor(squad$net_imp, squad$v_to) and cor(squad$con_impulse_rel, squad$v_to)
g <- 9.81 # 1. Take-off velocity from jump height (m/s) squad$v_to <- sqrt(2 * g * squad$jump_height_m) # 2. Net propulsive impulse per kg (N·s/kg) squad$net_imp <- squad$con_impulse_rel - g * squad$con_time_s # 3. Correlations r_physics <- cor(squad$net_imp, squad$v_to) r_gross <- cor(squad$con_impulse_rel, squad$v_to) c(r_physics = r_physics, r_gross = r_gross) 
g <- 9.81

# 1. Take-off velocity from jump height (m/s)
squad$v_to <- sqrt(2 * g * squad$jump_height_m)

# 2. Net propulsive impulse per kg (N·s/kg)
squad$net_imp <- squad$con_impulse_rel - g * squad$con_time_s

# 3. Correlations
r_physics <- cor(squad$net_imp, squad$v_to)
r_gross <- cor(squad$con_impulse_rel, squad$v_to)
c(r_physics = r_physics, r_gross = r_gross)

Plot it against the identity line:

The points scatter tightly around the identity line. So what does r = 0.96 mean here?

  • It does not mean “athletes who produce more impulse jump higher” as a physiological discovery. That’s Newton’s second law, true for every object.
  • It does tell you how well two calculation methods agree. The ~4 % of non-shared variance is measurement error and processing differences (how take-off and movement onset are detected, sampling rate, flight-time vs. impulse methods). In that sense, it’s a check of data quality, like the identity-line check in lesson 3.2.
ImportantThe take-home message

When two variables are two expressions of the same quantity (h and vTO, net impulse/mass and vTO), they share almost all of their variance by definition. Their correlation will be close to 1 no matter which athletes you test. It answers no research question about performance, and it should never be presented as a “finding”.

Case 3: partly shared components

Coupling isn’t always complete. Many derived variables share an ingredient:

Variable Calculated as Shares an ingredient with
Power force × velocity take-off velocity, and therefore jump height
RSImod jump height / contraction time jump height (numerator) and contraction time (denominator)
Relative impulse, force, power value / body mass body mass (denominator)

The correlation between RSImod and jump height (r = 0.66) is partly built in, because jump height is in RSImod. A simulation shows that this happens even with completely random, unrelated numbers:

Simulate 1000 “athletes” whose jump height h and contraction time t are independent random numbers. Then calculate the ratio rsi <- h / t and its correlations with h and with t.

rsi <- h / t, cor(rsi, h), cor(rsi, t).

set.seed(10) h <- rnorm(1000, mean = 0.40, sd = 0.05) # jump height (m) t <- rnorm(1000, mean = 0.80, sd = 0.10) # contraction time (s), unrelated to h rsi <- h / t r_h_t <- cor(h, t) # independent: close to 0 r_rsi_h <- cor(rsi, h) r_rsi_t <- cor(rsi, t) round(c(r_h_t = r_h_t, r_rsi_h = r_rsi_h, r_rsi_t = r_rsi_t), 2) 
set.seed(10)
h <- rnorm(1000, mean = 0.40, sd = 0.05)   # jump height (m)
t <- rnorm(1000, mean = 0.80, sd = 0.10)   # contraction time (s), unrelated to h

rsi <- h / t

r_h_t   <- cor(h, t)      # independent: close to 0
r_rsi_h <- cor(rsi, h)
r_rsi_t <- cor(rsi, t)
round(c(r_h_t = r_h_t, r_rsi_h = r_rsi_h, r_rsi_t = r_rsi_t), 2)

The same trap applies to ratio scaling by body mass: dividing by mass doesn’t necessarily remove the effect of size, and can even create a correlation with mass. In the squad, relative propulsive impulse (per kg) still correlates negatively with body mass:

Heavier athletes look “worse” per kg. Part of this is real physiology, but part is an artefact of dividing by mass when impulse doesn’t scale 1:1 with mass. Allometric scaling (dividing by mass raised to a power estimated from the data, e.g. m0.67) is the solution; you’ll fit such a model with log-transformed regression in the next lesson’s exercises.

A paper reports that “peak velocity was strongly related to jump height (r = 0.95)” in a group of volleyball players. What’s your critical comment?

When variables are mathematically or physically coupled, a high r is expected for any group of people, or even for a bouncing ball. Such correlations can validate a measurement method, but not a training hypothesis.

Which pair of variables is least affected by mathematical coupling?

RSImod contains jump height, and net impulse/mass equals take-off velocity by Newton’s law. Body mass and countermovement depth are measured independently, so any correlation between them reflects a genuine association (whatever its cause).

Correlation is not causation

Even when variables are measured independently, a correlation doesn’t show that one causes the other. Body mass correlates negatively with jump height (r = −0.37), but body mass also differs between the sports, and the sports differ in training, selection and playing demands. A third variable (a confounder) can create or hide a correlation. Multiple regression (lesson 4.8) lets you adjust for confounders, but only randomised experiments can establish causation convincingly.

When you read such a matrix, ask for every large value: are these two independent measurements, or do they share an ingredient?

r = −0.70 between countermovement depth (cmj_depth_m, negative = deeper) and propulsion time. What does r² tell you?

r² = 0.70² = 0.49. (The negative sign only means that more negative depth, i.e. deeper, goes with longer propulsion: a longer distance to push over.)

Lesson summary

  • cor(x, y) and cor.test(x, y): Pearson’s r; method = "spearman" for ranks/monotonic/outliers.
  • r² = shared variance.
  • Mathematical coupling: variables calculated from each other (h ↔︎ vTO), or two routes to the same physical quantity (net impulse/mass = vTO), share their variance by definition, so r ≈ 1 is not a finding. Ratios (RSI, per-kg values) create partial, built-in correlations.
  • Correlation ≠ causation: beware of confounders.
