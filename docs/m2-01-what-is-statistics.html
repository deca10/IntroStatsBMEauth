2.1 What is statistics?

Variability, descriptive vs. inferential statistics, and types of data

NoteIn this lesson you will learn to
  • explain what statistics is and why sport scientists need it,
  • tell descriptive from inferential statistics, and a sample from a population,
  • classify variables as continuous, discrete, nominal or ordinal, and store them correctly in R.

⏱️ About 30 minutes. Module 2 assumes you’ve completed Module 1.

Meet the new dataset: countermovement jumps

The countermovement jump (CMJ) is one of the most common tests in sport science. The athlete stands still, dips down quickly and jumps as high as possible. In this module you’ll work with real data from Athlete K, a professional basketball player who performed three CMJs on a dual force platform: one plate under each foot.

A force platform measures the ground reaction force (GRF), in newtons (N), 500 times per second (500 Hz). The data frame cmj has been loaded for you:

Column Meaning
trial CMJ 1, CMJ 2 or CMJ 3
time_s Time since the start of the recording (s); a new value every 0.002 s
force_left, force_right Vertical force under the left and right foot (N)
force_total Left + right (N)

Let’s plot one jump. You know how to make a line plot from lesson 1.5:

Reading the curve from left to right:

  1. Quiet standing (0–1.5 s): the force equals the athlete’s body weight (about 726 N).
  2. Unweighting: the athlete starts to dip, and the force drops below body weight.
  3. Braking and propulsion (≈2.0–2.6 s): the athlete stops the dip and pushes off, with force rising to about 2.5 × body weight.
  4. Flight: no contact, so the force is 0 N.
  5. Landing (≈3.2 s): a large impact peak, then the athlete settles back to body weight.

Each jump produces thousands of numbers. Statistics helps us turn them into a few meaningful ones.

What is statistics?

Statistics is the science of collecting, organising, summarising and interpreting data in order to answer questions and make decisions under uncertainty.

Why is there uncertainty? Because of variability. Measure the same thing twice and you get two different answers. Here are Athlete K’s three jumps, performed one minute apart:

The jump heights are 40.4, 39.6 and 41.3 cm. So what is Athlete K’s “real” jump height? If he jumps 42 cm next month, has he improved, or is that just normal variation? Questions like these are what statistics is for. Other examples from sport and exercise science:

  • Does a 6-week plyometric programme improve jump height more than regular training?
  • Is the left leg producing more force than the right (asymmetry)?
  • Do older adults with a history of falls have worse balance?
  • What is a “normal” Mini-BESTest score for a 70-year-old?

Athlete K’s three jump heights differ (40.4, 39.6 and 41.3 cm). What is the best explanation?

Variability is normal and everywhere: biological variation (fatigue, technique, motivation) plus measurement error. Statistics gives us tools to describe it and to decide whether a difference is bigger than this normal variation.

Descriptive and inferential statistics

Statistics has two main branches.

Descriptive statistics summarise the data you actually have: averages, ranges, tables and plots. “Athlete K’s mean jump height over these three trials was 40.4 cm.” “The 163 participants in the balance study were aged 18 to 86 years.” No conclusions beyond the data are drawn.

Inferential statistics use a sample to draw conclusions about a larger population, and say how certain those conclusions are. “Older adults score lower on the Mini-BESTest than younger adults (p < 0.001).” This is a claim about all older and younger adults, based on only 163 of them.

Two pairs of terms go with this distinction:

Term Meaning Example
Population Everyone (or everything) you want to draw conclusions about All community-dwelling adults; every jump Athlete K could ever make
Sample The part of the population you actually measured The 163 participants in the balance study; Athlete K’s 3 jumps
Parameter A number describing the population (usually unknown) The true mean Mini-BESTest score of all older adults
Statistic A number calculated from the sample, used to estimate the parameter The mean Mini-BESTest score of the 76 older participants

This module focuses on descriptive statistics, plus two key ideas (the normal distribution and the central limit theorem) that form the bridge to inferential statistics in Module 4.

Calculate a descriptive statistic: Athlete K’s mean jump height across the three trials. Use the height_cm column of jumps.

Select the column with $ and use mean(): mean(jumps$height_cm).

# Mean jump height of Athlete K mean(jumps$height_cm) 
# Mean jump height of Athlete K
mean(jumps$height_cm)

“Based on our sample of 40 players, U18 basketball players in Greece jump on average 38 cm (95 % CI 36–40 cm).” Which type of statistics is this?

The statement draws a conclusion about all U18 players in Greece (the population) from 40 players (a sample), and expresses the uncertainty with a confidence interval. That’s inference.

“In our study, 42 of the 163 participants reported at least one fall in the past year.” Which type of statistics is this?

It simply reports what happened in the sample, with no generalisation to a population.

A researcher measures the VO₂max of 25 university rowers to learn about the fitness of university rowers in general. What are the population and the sample?

The population is the whole group you want to know about; the sample is the subset you actually measured.

Data type classification

Before you summarise or analyse a variable, you must know what type of data it is, because that determines which statistics and plots make sense. For example, a mean is meaningful for jump height but not for playing position.

Type Sub-type Description Sport-science examples Store in R as
Numerical (quantitative) Continuous Measured; any value in a range (decimals possible) jump height, force, body mass, sprint time, VO₂max numeric
Discrete Counted; whole numbers only number of falls, goals, repetitions, injuries per season numeric / integer
Categorical (qualitative) Nominal Categories with no natural order sex, sport, playing position, left/right leg factor
Ordinal Categories with a natural order activity level (low < moderate < high), RPE category, injury severity ordered factor
TipWatch out for numbers that are really categories

A participant ID (1, 2, 3, …) or a shirt number is stored as a number, but it’s really a nominal label: an “average shirt number” means nothing. Similarly, scale scores like the Mini-BESTest (0–28) or a Likert item (1–5) are technically ordinal, although sum scores like the Mini-BESTest are often treated as numerical in practice.

Let’s look at the balance dataset with this lens:

Column Type
age, height_cm, mass_kg, bmi, tmt_a_s, tmt_b_s continuous
falls_12m discrete (a count)
sex, nationality, illness, age_group nominal
activity, fear_falling ordinal
subject an ID label (nominal), not a quantity

Now classify some variables yourself.

The peak force during a CMJ (in N) is…

Force is measured on a continuous scale and can take any value, e.g. 1888.2 N.

The number of successful free throws out of 10 is…

It’s a count, so it can only be a whole number from 0 to 10.

Rating of perceived exertion recorded as very light / light / moderate / hard / very hard is…

These are categories with a clear order, but the “distance” between them is not necessarily equal. That’s the hallmark of ordinal data.

The trial column of cmj (CMJ 1, CMJ 2, CMJ 3) is best treated as…

“CMJ 1/2/3” is a label. You might argue for ordinal (trials happened in order), but you wouldn’t calculate an average trial. It identifies groups of measurements.

Lesson summary

  • Statistics turns data into answers while accounting for variability.
  • Descriptive statistics summarise the data you have; inferential statistics generalise from a sample to a population.
  • Numerical data is continuous (measured) or discrete (counted); categorical data is nominal (no order) or ordinal (ordered).
  • In R: numbers → numeric, nominal → factor(), ordinal → factor(..., ordered = TRUE, levels = ...).
