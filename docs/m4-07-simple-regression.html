4.7 Simple linear regression

Fitting a line, interpreting it, predicting with it, and log-log models for scaling

NoteIn this lesson you will learn to
  • fit a simple linear regression with lm() and interpret the intercept and slope,
  • read the summary() output: standard errors, t, p, R², residual SE,
  • predict new values, with confidence and prediction intervals,
  • check residuals,
  • recognise “perfect” models built on coupled variables,
  • use a log–log regression to find how a variable scales with body mass (allometry).

⏱️ About 50 minutes.

From correlation to regression

Correlation tells you how strongly two variables are related. Regression describes how they’re related: it fits the straight line that best predicts an outcome y from a predictor x:

\[y = b_0 + b_1 x + \varepsilon\]

  • b₀, the intercept: predicted y when x = 0,
  • b₁, the slope: the change in y for a 1-unit increase in x,
  • ε, the residual: the part of y the line doesn’t explain.

“Best” means least squares: the line that minimises the sum of the squared residuals, \(\sum e_i^2 = \sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2\), where \(\hat{y}_i = b_0 + b_1 x_i\) is the predicted value. Minimising it gives closed formulas for the two coefficients:

\[b_1 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \bar{x})^2} = r \, \frac{s_y}{s_x} \qquad\qquad b_0 = \bar{y} - b_1 \bar{x}\]

So the slope is the correlation rescaled into the units of y per unit of x, and the line always passes through the point \((\bar{x}, \bar{y})\).

Fitting a model with lm()

The formula reads “jump height is modelled by peak power”. The coefficients:

  • Intercept = −0.018 m: the predicted jump height at 0 W/kg. That’s a meaningless extrapolation far outside the data, and that’s fine: the intercept just anchors the line.
  • Slope = 0.0076 m per W/kg: each extra 1 W/kg of peak power goes with 0.76 cm more jump height, on average.

The summary() output

  • Coefficients table: each estimate with its standard error, t value and p-value (H₀: coefficient = 0). For the slope: \[SE(b_1) = \frac{s_e}{\sqrt{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2}}, \qquad t = \frac{b_1}{SE(b_1)}, \qquad df = n - 2\] \[95\%\ CI = b_1 \pm t^*_{n-2}\, SE(b_1)\]
  • Residual standard error (0.031 m): the typical size of a residual, i.e. how far athletes are from the line, about ±3 cm. Two coefficients were estimated, so it divides by n − 2: \[s_e = \sqrt{\frac{\sum e_i^2}{n - 2}} = \sqrt{\frac{SS_{residual}}{n - 2}}\]
  • Multiple R-squared (0.69): the proportion of variance in y explained by the model. In simple regression it equals r². \[R^2 = 1 - \frac{SS_{residual}}{SS_{total}} = 1 - \frac{\sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2}\]
  • F-statistic: tests the model as a whole, \(F = \dfrac{(SS_{total} - SS_{residual}) / 1}{SS_{residual} / (n - 2)}\). In simple regression \(F = t^2\) for the slope, with the same p.

Useful extractor functions: coef(model), confint(model) (95 % CIs of the coefficients), summary(model)$r.squared, residuals(model), fitted(model).

Fit a regression predicting jump height from countermovement depth (cmj_depth_m). Store it in model, then extract the slope (slope, the second coefficient) and R² (r2).

lm(jump_height_m ~ cmj_depth_m, data = squad), coef(model)[2], summary(model)$r.squared.

Predicting

predict() applies the model to new data. Supply a data frame with the predictor column:

The two intervals answer different questions:

  • confidence interval (39.1–40.4 cm): where is the average jump height of all athletes with 55 W/kg?
  • prediction interval (33.5–46.0 cm): where will a single new athlete with 55 W/kg probably land? It’s much wider, because it includes the individual scatter around the line.

For a new value \(x_0\), with prediction \(\hat{y}_0 = b_0 + b_1 x_0\):

\[\text{CI: } \hat{y}_0 \pm t^*_{n-2}\, s_e \sqrt{\frac{1}{n} + \frac{(x_0 - \bar{x})^2}{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2}}\]

\[\text{PI: } \hat{y}_0 \pm t^*_{n-2}\, s_e \sqrt{1 + \frac{1}{n} + \frac{(x_0 - \bar{x})^2}{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2}}\]

The extra “1 +” in the prediction interval is the individual scatter. Both intervals widen as \(x_0\) moves away from \(\bar{x}\), which is one more reason not to extrapolate.

A coach uses the model to predict the jump height of a 14-year-old with 30 W/kg peak power (the squad ranged from about 40 to 80 W/kg). What’s the problem?

Regression models are only trustworthy within the range (and the population) they were fitted on.

Checking the residuals

Linear regression assumes that the residuals:

  1. show no pattern against the fitted values (the relationship is linear),
  2. have roughly constant spread (homoscedasticity),
  3. are roughly normal,
  4. are independent (one observation per athlete).

The quickest check is a plot of residuals against fitted values: you want a shapeless cloud around 0.

(plot(model) in RStudio shows four standard diagnostic plots at once.)

A “perfect” model that explains nothing

Remember mathematical coupling from lesson 4.6? Regression doesn’t protect you from it:

R² = 0.997, a near-perfect model, and completely useless: you can’t “explain” jump height with a variable that was calculated from jump height. A high R² only means something when the predictor is measured independently of the outcome.

A student builds a model predicting jump height from RSImod and is happy with R² = 0.43. What should they consider?

The outcome appears inside the predictor. Part of the “explained” variance is the outcome explaining itself.

Log–log regression and allometric scaling

Many quantities don’t scale 1:1 with body size. Allometry describes this with a power law:

\[Y = a \cdot M^{b}\]

where M is body mass and b the scaling exponent. Taking logarithms turns it into a straight line:

\[\log(Y) = \log(a) + b \cdot \log(M)\]

So a regression of log(Y) on log(M) estimates b as its slope. Dividing by body mass (“per kg” values) silently assumes b = 1. If the true b is smaller, per-kg values will penalise heavier athletes, which is exactly the negative correlation between relative impulse and body mass you saw in lesson 4.6.

  1. Recover the absolute propulsive impulse (N·s) by multiplying con_impulse_rel by body_mass_kg, and store it in squad$impulse.
  2. Fit allo_model: log(impulse) on log(body_mass_kg).
  3. Extract the scaling exponent b (the slope) and its 95 % CI b_ci with confint(). Does the CI include 1?

squad$con_impulse_rel * squad$body_mass_kg, then lm(log(impulse) ~ log(body_mass_kg), data = squad).

In lm(log(Y) ~ log(M)), the slope is 0.67. What happens if you express Y per kg body mass (Y/M)?

Y/M ∝ M0.67−1 = M−0.33, which falls with mass. Divide by M0.67 instead.

Lesson summary

  • lm(y ~ x, data = df) fits a line; the slope is the change in y per unit of x.
  • summary() gives coefficients ± SE, p-values, residual SE and R² (= r² in simple regression).
  • predict(model, newdata, interval = "confidence" / "prediction").
  • Check residuals vs. fitted values; don’t extrapolate.
  • A high R² with a coupled predictor is meaningless.
  • Log–log regression estimates allometric exponents: per-kg values are only fair when b ≈ 1.
