2.2 Measures of center

Mean, median and mode, and when to use which

NoteIn this lesson you will learn to
  • describe the “typical” value of a variable with the mean, median and mode,
  • calculate them by hand and with R,
  • decide whether the mean or the median is the better summary, depending on the shape of the data,
  • deal with missing values (NA).

⏱️ About 35 minutes.

Measures of center

When you have many numbers, the first question is usually: what is a typical value? A measure of center (or central tendency) answers that with a single number. The three most common are:

Measure Definition In R
Mean The sum of all values divided by how many there are (the “average”) mean(x)
Median The middle value once the data are sorted: half the values lie below it, half above median(x)
Mode The most frequent value no built-in function; use table(x)

The mode is mainly useful for categorical data (“the most common playing position”). For numerical data, the mean and median are what you’ll use almost every time, so they’re the focus of this lesson.

Mean and median

The mean

The mean of n values \(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n\) is

\[\bar{x} = \frac{x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_n}{n} = \frac{\sum x_i}{n}\]

You can calculate it step by step with sum() and length() (which counts the elements), or directly with mean():

The median

To find the median, sort the values and take the middle one:

With an even number of values there are two middle values, and the median is their average:

Real data: body weight from a force platform

Here is a classic sport-science use of the mean. Before a CMJ the athlete stands still, and the force platform measures their body weight. But the signal is never perfectly flat: the person sways slightly and the sensors have a little noise. The solution is to take the mean force during quiet standing.

Missing values

Real datasets have gaps. In R a missing value is written NA (not available). By default, mean() and median() return NA if any value is missing, which is R’s way of saying “I can’t be sure”. Add na.rm = TRUE (remove NAs) to ignore them:

What is the median of c(4, 1, 9, 6)?

Sorted: 1 4 6 9. With an even number of values, the median is the mean of the two middle values: (4 + 6) / 2 = 5. (The mean would be 20 / 4 = 5 too, but that’s a coincidence!)

What is the most appropriate “center” for playing position (guard, forward, center)?

Playing position is nominal, so it has no numbers to average and no order to find a middle. The only measure that makes sense is the most frequent category, the mode.

Mean vs. median

For a symmetric distribution, the mean and median are almost the same. They differ when the data are skewed or contain extreme values, because:

  • the mean uses the actual size of every value, so a single extreme value pulls it towards itself,
  • the median only uses the order of the values, so it is robust: extreme values barely affect it.

One bad trial drops the mean by 5.6 cm, while the median changes by only 1 cm.

Skewed data

A distribution is right-skewed (positively skewed) when most values are low but a long tail stretches to the right, and left-skewed when the tail is on the left. In a skewed distribution the mean is pulled towards the tail:

Shape Order
Symmetric mean ≈ median
Right-skewed (tail to the right) mean > median
Left-skewed (tail to the left) mean < median

The Trail Making Test B time is strongly right-skewed: most participants finished within about 2 minutes, but some needed far longer (the test stops at 300 s). The histogram shows the mean (red) and median (blue):

geom_vline() draws a vertical line at the value given by xintercept.

A surprise in the force data

Now let’s compare the mean and median of the whole force recording of CMJ 3, from quiet standing through the jump and landing:

The mean force over the whole trial (≈727 N) equals body weight, even though the force went from 0 N (flight) up to more than 5700 N (landing)! This is no coincidence. The athlete starts and ends standing still, so (by Newton’s laws, the impulse–momentum relationship) the force above body weight and the force below body weight must exactly balance out over the trial. The mean “sees” the size of every value, so it finds that balance point.

The median just reports the middle value of all the samples (≈725 N). In other jumps, where the athlete spent more or less time at different force levels, the median can be far from body weight. Try median(subset(cmj, trial == "CMJ 2")$force_total).

Use tapply() to calculate the median Mini-BESTest score for each age_group. tapply(values, groups, function) applies a function to the values of each group.

tapply(balance$minibest, balance$age_group, median). Note that the function name is given without brackets.

The annual salaries in a basketball team are: eleven players around €40,000 and one star player at €1,500,000. Which value best describes the typical salary?

The star’s salary is an extreme value that drags the mean up to about €160,000, a salary nobody on the team actually earns. The median (≈€40,000) represents the typical player.

A histogram of reaction times has a long tail to the right. Which statement is most likely true?

In a right-skewed distribution, the few large values pull the mean towards the tail, above the median.

When should you prefer the mean over the median?

For symmetric data, the mean and median are similar, but the mean has useful mathematical properties (it’s the basis for SD, t-tests, ANOVA…). Use the median for skewed data or data with outliers, or report both.

Lesson summary

  • Mean = sum / n (mean()); median = middle value of the sorted data (median()); mode = most frequent value (table()), mostly for categorical data.
  • Use na.rm = TRUE when data contain NA.
  • The mean is sensitive to extreme values; the median is robust.
  • Symmetric data: mean ≈ median. Right-skewed: mean > median. Left-skewed: mean < median.
  • tapply(values, groups, function) calculates a statistic per group.
