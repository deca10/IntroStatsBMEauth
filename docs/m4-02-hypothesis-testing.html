4.2 Hypothesis testing

Null hypotheses, p-values, errors, power, and statistical vs. practical significance

NoteIn this lesson you will learn to
  • formulate a null and an alternative hypothesis,
  • explain what a p-value is (and what it isn’t),
  • run a one-sample t-test with t.test(),
  • describe type I and type II errors and statistical power,
  • distinguish statistical from practical significance.

⏱️ About 40 minutes.

The logic of a hypothesis test

A strength coach claims: “Professional athletes in our league jump 40 cm on average.” Our squad of 84 athletes has a mean of:

That’s 39.3 cm, not 40 cm. But samples always differ a bit from the population. Is this difference larger than what sampling variability alone would produce? A hypothesis test answers this in four steps:

  1. State the hypotheses.
    • Null hypothesis H₀: nothing special is going on. The population mean is 0.40 m.
    • Alternative hypothesis H₁: the population mean is not 0.40 m.
  2. Calculate a test statistic that measures how far the data are from H₀, in units of standard error. For a mean, that’s the t-statistic: \[t = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{SE}\]
  3. Find the p-value: the probability of getting a t at least this extreme if H₀ were true.
  4. Decide: if p is smaller than a pre-chosen significance level α (usually 0.05), reject H₀ (“statistically significant”). Otherwise, you fail to reject H₀.

The one-sample t-test

t.test(x, mu = value) tests whether the mean of x differs from value:

The output gives the t-statistic, the degrees of freedom (n − 1), the p-value, the 95 % CI and the sample mean. Here p ≈ 0.29 > 0.05, so we cannot reject the coach’s claim. A 0.7 cm difference is easily explained by sampling variability.

Notice that 0.40 m lies inside the 95 % CI. That’s no coincidence: a two-sided test at α = 0.05 is significant exactly when the hypothesised value lies outside the 95 % CI.

A physiotherapist states that the average mean propulsive force (con_force_rel) of professional athletes is 20 N/kg. Test this claim with a one-sample t-test on the squad data. Store the result in res, then print the p-value with res$p.value.

t.test(squad$con_force_rel, mu = 20)

res <- t.test(squad$con_force_rel, mu = 20) res res$p.value

What a p-value is, and what it isn’t

The p-value is: the probability of observing data at least as extreme as ours, assuming H₀ is true.

A small p means the data would be surprising if H₀ were true. Common misinterpretations:

❌ Wrong ✅ Why
“p = 0.03 means there’s a 3 % chance H₀ is true.” p is calculated assuming H₀ is true; it can’t be the probability of H₀.
“p > 0.05 proves there’s no effect.” Absence of evidence isn’t evidence of absence. The study may simply be too small.
“A smaller p means a bigger effect.” p depends on effect size and sample size. A tiny effect in a huge sample can give a tiny p.
“p < 0.05 means the result is important.” Statistical significance ≠ practical importance (see below).

A study finds p = 0.20 for the difference in sprint time between two training groups. What can you conclude?

Failing to reject H₀ isn’t proof of H₀. Look at the CI of the difference: if it’s wide, the study is inconclusive.

Two kinds of errors

Every decision can be wrong in two ways:

H₀ is actually true H₀ is actually false
Reject H₀ ❌ Type I error (false positive), probability α ✅ correct (probability = power)
Don’t reject H₀ ✅ correct ❌ Type II error (false negative), probability β

α is the risk you accept of declaring an effect that doesn’t exist. With α = 0.05, 1 in 20 tests of a true null hypothesis will be “significant” by chance. Let’s check that with a simulation: two groups drawn from the same population, so H₀ is true:

About 5 % false positives, as promised. Now imagine a study measuring 20 different variables, each tested at α = 0.05: you’d expect one “significant” result even if nothing is going on. This is the multiple comparisons problem, and it’s why fishing through many variables for p < 0.05 is bad practice.

Statistical power

Power (1 − β) is the probability that a test detects an effect that really exists. It depends on:

  • the effect size (bigger effects are easier to detect),
  • the sample size (more athletes → more power),
  • the variability of the measurements (a more reliable test → more power),
  • α.

Studies in sport science often have small samples and therefore low power: they may miss real effects, and the significant effects they do find tend to be overestimated.

Estimate power by simulation. Suppose a training programme truly improves jump height by 3 cm (from 40 to 43 cm, SD 5 cm). With 20 athletes per group, how often would a t-test detect it?

Complete the code: the second group’s mean should be 43. power_20 is the proportion of the 1000 simulated studies with p < 0.05.

mean = 43 and power_20 <- mean(p_values < 0.05).

set.seed(42) p_values <- replicate(1000, t.test(rnorm(20, mean = 40, sd = 5), rnorm(20, mean = 43, sd = 5))$p.value) power_20 <- mean(p_values < 0.05) power_20 
set.seed(42)
p_values <- replicate(1000, t.test(rnorm(20, mean = 40, sd = 5),
                                   rnorm(20, mean = 43, sd = 5))$p.value)
power_20 <- mean(p_values < 0.05)
power_20

A study with 8 athletes per group finds no significant effect of a supplement (p = 0.31). What’s the most likely issue?

With 8 per group, only large effects can be detected reliably. Report the effect size with its CI, and plan sample sizes with a power analysis before collecting data.

Statistical vs. practical significance

With enough data, any tiny difference becomes “statistically significant”. The question a coach cares about is: is the difference big enough to matter?

Imagine 5,000 athletes per group with a true difference of 0.3 cm in jump height:

p is small, but 0.3 cm is less than the trial-to-trial variation of a single athlete. It’s practically irrelevant. Always report and interpret:

  1. the effect size in meaningful units (cm, %, s) or as a standardised effect (Cohen’s d, lesson 4.3),
  2. its confidence interval,
  3. whether it exceeds the smallest worthwhile change for your context (lesson 4.9).

A p-value says nothing about the size of the effect. 4 milliseconds over 30 m is smaller than the timing gates’ precision.

Lesson summary

  • Hypothesis test: H₀ vs. H₁ → test statistic → p-value → compare with α.
  • t.test(x, mu = value): one-sample t-test. Results: $statistic, $p.value, $conf.int, $estimate.
  • p = P(data this extreme | H₀ true). It’s not the probability that H₀ is true, and not a measure of effect size.
  • Type I error = false positive (α); type II error = false negative (β); power = 1 − β.
  • Report effect sizes with CIs and judge practical relevance, not just p < 0.05.
